Width or Interaction Fidelity?

A note on my ICLR 2027 submission: how a continual learner that carries only a handful of statistics should spend a fixed memory budget — and why the honest answer to the title is "it depends, and here is on what."

This is a reading companion to the paper, written the way I would explain it at a whiteboard. It builds up from the setup, so you can follow it without the appendix open. Every number here is from the submission.

Learning without looking back

The setting is class-incremental continual learning: a model meets new classes over a stream of tasks, and once a task is gone, its data and even its label are unavailable. Most methods fight the resulting forgetting with a replay buffer or repeated gradient updates.

Analytic continual learners take a different route. They keep a pretrained backbone frozen and fit only a linear ridge readout — a classifier with a closed-form solution. The useful fact is that this readout depends on all the past data through just two running sums: a Gram matrix G, which records how the features co-vary, and a feature–label statistic S. Add a new task, update the two sums, re-solve in closed form. No stored examples, no gradients.

One more trick raises accuracy: expand the frozen features into a much larger set of m random coordinates, which makes classes easier to separate. More coordinates, better accuracy. So far, width looks free.

Why width is expensive

It is not free, because width is paid for in the state the learner must carry from task to task — what we call the continuation state. The feature–label statistic and the diagonal of the Gram grow linearly with width. But the Gram's off-diagonal part — how every coordinate interacts with every other — grows quadratically. At m = 10,000 coordinates that matrix alone is about 381 MiB in single precision.

So whenever this state has to stay small — picture many task streams each keeping their own readout on one shared frozen backbone — the quadratic interaction term, not the coordinates themselves, is what caps how wide a learner can afford to be.

The question nobody had isolated

That sets up a clean trade-off that the literature had always bundled with other differences. Fix a memory budget. You can spend it two ways:

Keep exact interactions among fewer coordinates — or keep more coordinates and store how they interact only approximately.

Earlier methods either stored the full Gram of one representation or compressed it at a single fixed width, and comparisons across them mixed training recipes in with state sizes. So it stayed genuinely open: at equal memory, is width worth more than interaction fidelity, and when? The figure shows the two ways of spending the same budget.

The idea: keep the cheap things exact

The method we study, DPSR (Diagonal-Preserving Spectral Ridge), is the simplest honest realization of that allocation. It keeps the things that are cheap — the feature–label statistic and each coordinate's own energy (the Gram's diagonal) — exact, because they only grow linearly. It compresses just the expensive off-diagonal interactions into a rank-bounded factor, restores the discarded diagonal energies, and then solves a full-width classifier in closed form with a standard matrix identity.

I want to be clear about what DPSR is and is not. Its pieces are all established; the contribution is the exact per-coordinate bookkeeping that makes it a transparent probe of the trade-off, not a new compression trick competing for a leaderboard. The point was to change one thing at a time — width versus interaction rank — with everything else held fixed, so the comparison actually measures what it claims to.

(The paper draws a loose analogy to the fruit-fly mushroom body, where a sparse random expansion also precedes a simple readout. It is a conceptual picture only, not a model of the circuit.)

What we found

First, the compression itself is nearly free in accuracy. On three class-incremental benchmarks with a frozen ViT-B/16, DPSR stays within 0.1 points of a full-Gram readout of the same expansion while storing 88–89% less continuation state.

Second, the headline question. At equal budgets, trading interaction rank for more width beat exact ridge on a narrower expansion in 17 of 18 configurations — by 0.17 to 2.26 points — when the ridge penalty was chosen by generalized cross-validation. In the one remaining case, where too little interaction rank was left, width lost by 0.68 points. So the answer is conditional: spending the budget on width pays while enough interaction rank remains, and how much is enough depends on the dataset.

Third, and this is the part I would not hide from a reviewer: the size of the win depends on how you pick the penalty. Under a different, equally reasonable selection rule (validation and refit), the gains shrink, are clearest only on CIFAR-100, and are matched there by other corrected sketches — so we do not claim the benefit is specific to DPSR. We also watched interaction fidelity decay along the stream: a fixed-rank factor captures a shrinking share of each coordinate's energy as tasks pile up, and with too little rank (256 on CIFAR-100) the deficit to the full Gram grows to 8.38 points by the last stage.

Where it breaks

Two honest caveats carry the paper's real contribution as much as the wins do. There is a failure mode — selecting the penalty before truncating the state — that collapses first-task accuracy on CUB-200 in two of three seeds, for several methods including DPSR. And the savings are in persistent state, not compute or peak memory: DPSR's total state can even exceed a narrow exact baseline's. The study uses three seeds without multiplicity correction and only frozen features. These are the boundaries of the claim, and naming them is the point.

Why I care, and what is next

This project is why I want to do a PhD on resource-aware learning. It left me with concrete questions. If the rank a learner needs is data-dependent and drifts as tasks accumulate, can a learner detect that from signals it already has and reallocate on the fly? Can penalty selection be made refit-aware, closing the failure mode above? Does the same budget view — decide which interactions are worth keeping — extend past frozen features to trainable representations, and even to the fixed-size state of large models, like the key–value cache of a transformer or the recurrent state of a state-space model? That last one is a hypothesis, not a result, and it is the direction I most want to test.
